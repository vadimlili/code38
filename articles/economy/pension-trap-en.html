Demography vs the state

Ukraine’s pension trap: why people in their 30s and 40s should rely only on their own investments

Demographic collapse, the bankruptcy of the pay-as-you-go system and a practical savings strategy for a generation to whom the state pension no longer guarantees anything.

≈ 14 min read Analysis Pension system · Investment Author: vadim.li · vadim.li@gmail.com

The text may be used only with the author’s permission. For inquiries and citations: vadim.li@gmail.com

The macroeconomic stability of a pay-as-you-go pension system fundamentally depends on a demographic pyramid in which the working-age population far outnumbers people of pension age. In Ukraine this ratio has been irreversibly destroyed by a combination of long-term demographic trends and a full-scale war.

The state has already effectively acknowledged the limit of its capabilities. The question is no longer whether there will be enough money for a decent pension — it is what a person of 30–40, who should no longer count on the pay-as-you-go system, ought to do.

Part 01

Demographic collapse as a fundamental constraint on the economy

According to estimates by the Ptoukha Institute for Demography and Social Studies of the National Academy of Sciences of Ukraine, the country’s cumulative demographic losses have reached 10 million people. This figure covers not only irrecoverable losses and occupied territories, but also mass emigration, deaths exceeding births and unrealized demographic potential. Roughly 31.5 million people currently live in territory controlled by the Ukrainian government.

Forced emigration has become the key factor destabilizing the labor force. According to Eurostat and international organizations, about 5 million Ukrainian refugees are abroad, 4.3 million of them registered in EU countries and another 700,000 in the US, the UK, Canada and elsewhere. The demographic structure of the emigration creates a long-term risk: about 33% of migrants are children and teenagers under 18, while those aged 65 and over make up no more than 6%. Since European countries and private businesses have a systematic interest in absorbing an educated and adaptable Ukrainian workforce, a large share of young people will not return. After martial law is lifted, a second wave of emigration is expected — of men seeking to reunite with families abroad.

At the same time the birth rate is collapsing. In 2021 the fertility rate was about 1.2 children per woman; by 2025 it had fallen to an extreme level of about 0.7. Simple generational replacement requires 2.15. Even in an optimistic post-war scenario the rate would recover only to 1.6.

The country is forming an inverted age pyramid in which a small young generation will be unable to generate tax revenue sufficient even for basic social obligations.

Part 02

The bankruptcy of the pay-as-you-go system: the state guarantees only a minimum

A pay-as-you-go pension system works on the principle of immediate redistribution: money paid by the working population as the unified social contribution (USC) goes straight to paying current pensioners. Under a catastrophic demographic imbalance this model loses its financial viability.

Falling birth rate → fewer future contributors → heavier burden per worker → Pension Fund deficit → pensions fall behind inflation

According to the Pension Fund of Ukraine, there are about 10.2 million pensioners. At the end of 2021 there were 10 million pensioners for 15.5 million workers (a ratio of 1.55 to 1); now the ratio has approached the critical 1:1. Pension Fund spending in 2025 amounted to UAH 799 billion, requiring constant subsidies from the state budget. Meanwhile the average pension in Ukraine is only UAH 6,437.

“In the future the pay-as-you-go pension will only be able to guarantee the minimum social level needed to protect against poverty” — Daria Marchak, First Deputy Minister of Social Policy of Ukraine.

The government’s reform plan envisages a move to a points system modeled on Germany and Austria: contributions will be converted into standardized points whose value will depend on the Pension Fund’s financial position. The reform will abolish more than 160 types of state supplements but does not solve the shortage of resources. Meanwhile the required insurance record keeps rising: in 2025 retiring at 60 required 32 years of contributions, in 2026 — 33, and by 2028 the threshold will reach 35 years.

For people aged 30–40 this means a harsh reality: even with 35 years of official employment and conscientious payment of all taxes, the state pension in 2045–2055 will cover no more than 20–25% of their usual working income.

Part 03

Non-state pension funds (NPFs): a domestic savings instrument

Voluntary non-state pension provision (the third pillar) has existed in Ukraine for more than 20 years and is the first accessible alternative instrument for building your own capital. According to industry associations, there are 50 non-state pension funds in the country with about 900,000 participants. Over their lifetime they have raised almost UAH 3.5 billion in contributions, paid out more than UAH 2 billion in pensions, and earned a cumulative investment income of UAH 5.5 billion.

The most successful private funds (Dynasty, OTP Pension, PrivatFond, Enerit-Ukraine) show average annual returns of 13.9%–18.7% in hryvnia, exceeding inflation, devaluation and bank deposit rates. The contributor base has changed: at the start employers made 97% of contributions; now voluntary contributions from individuals exceed corporate payments.

Tax advantages

Officially employed people who pay personal income tax can claim back 18% of their contributions to an NPF each year through a tax credit. Unlike deposits, where income is taxed every year, investment income in an NPF is reinvested without interim taxation.

On payout, personal income tax (18%) and the military levy are withheld from only 60% of each payment. After age 70, payments are fully tax-exempt.

The calculations show how critically the size of savings depends on the age at which you start investing. For passive income of UAH 20,000 a month (adjusted for inflation) from age 60 to 80, the following monthly contributions are required:

Part 04

Global stock markets: investing through foreign brokers

Accumulating capital only in the national currency carries high sovereign, inflation and devaluation risks. For people aged 30–40, currency diversification through global stock markets in hard currency (USD/EUR) is a strategic necessity.

The main international broker that still opens accounts for and serves Ukrainian residents is Interactive Brokers (IBKR) — a US public company regulated by the SEC, the CFTC (US), the FCA (UK) and other global regulators.

National Bank currency restrictions and routing funds

  • Funding brokerage accounts directly from Ukrainian bank cards is prohibited
  • Transfers to accounts abroad are capped at UAH 100,000 a month
  • Investors use international payment systems and European neobanks (Wise, Revolut, ZEN): hryvnia → neobank within the National Bank limit → SEPA transfer (in euros) to IBKR’s account details. SEPA fees start at €0.10, much cheaper than a classic SWIFT transfer

Interactive Brokers trading terms

  • Minimum deposit: from $0
  • Trading commission: $0.005 per US share (minimum $1, maximum 1% of the trade value)
  • Withdrawals: the first transaction each month is free, each subsequent one costs $10
  • Core instruments: diversified ETFs — Vanguard S&P 500 (VOO), iShares Core S&P 500 (IVV), Vanguard Total World Stock (VT), iShares Core MSCI World (URTH)

Taxation of income from foreign investments

Ukrainian law requires residents to declare income from foreign brokers themselves — IBKR is not a Ukrainian tax agent. The annual declaration of assets and income is filed by May 1.

Investment gains: the difference between the sale and purchase price, taxed at 18% personal income tax + 5% military levy (23% in total). Dividends: 9% income tax + 5% military levy (14% in total). Under the W-8BEN treaty form, the 15% US withholding tax on dividends is credited against Ukrainian income tax — no additional income tax is due, but the 5% military levy must be paid.

The compound-interest effect

Capital growth is described by the formula for compounding regular contributions: final capital depends on the monthly contribution, the monthly rate of return and the number of months invested.

Worked example

With a monthly contribution of $300 at 8% a year in dollars, an investor who starts at 30 (a 30-year horizon, to age 60) accumulates ~$447,000 — own contributions total $108,000, and the remaining $339,000 (75.8%) is generated by compound interest.

An investor who starts at 40 (a 20-year horizon) accumulates only ~$177,000 — $72,000 in contributions and $105,000 (59.3%) in investment gains.

Delaying the start of investing by 10 years, from 30 to 40, cuts final capital by a factor of 2.5 — while own contributions differ by only 33%.

Part 06

Conclusions and a strategic action plan

The demographic crisis, the loss of 10 million citizens to war and emigration, a fertility rate down to 0.7 and the Pension Fund’s chronic deficit prove that the collapse of Ukraine’s pay-as-you-go system is irreversible. For today’s 30- and 40-year-olds the state will be able to provide only basic social assistance at subsistence level. To maintain their accustomed standard of living after their working years, citizens need an independent investment strategy.

A practical action plan

  • A diversified portfolio: 70% of free savings in global index ETFs through a foreign broker (IBKR) in hard currency, transferred via neobanks within National Bank limits. The remaining 30% in local instruments (an NPF for the tax credit and FX-denominated government bonds)
  • Systematic saving: set aside at least 15–20% of monthly income for investment before current consumer spending
  • Tax discipline: an annual declaration of assets and income, correct records of securities purchase costs, use of W-8BEN treaty benefits

Time is an investor’s main resource: starting to build your own capital at 30–40 is the only way to secure financial independence in later life.

Foreign signs vs our own capital

An economy of cashiers or a nation of creators?

The bitter truth about global brands and what real investment is.

Let’s be frank. Every time another multinational chain enters our market — trendy coffee shops, Swedish mass-market fashion or American fast food — society erupts in genuine euphoria. News feeds fill with happy selfies, officials cheerfully cut ribbons and report a “tremendous success and an improved investment climate.”

Emotionally, this is understandable. For many it is a marker of belonging to the civilized world. But set the emotions aside and look at the movement of capital, and you have to admit an unpopular truth: often we are celebrating having an economic vacuum cleaner hung around our neck.

It is time to learn to separate two completely different things:

Multinational expansion Investment in local business
What happensA global giant opens its branches hereForeign funds and banks invest in our companies
Where the profit goes↗ To headquarters abroad↺ Reinvested in the country
Where decisions are madeIn the brand’s home countryAt home
Bottom line✕ Often pumps money out of us✓ Makes us rich

Let’s break it down.

Part 01

Why the arrival of corporations is not always a celebration

When a global giant opens branches here, it is not building our economy. It is scaling its own. And for the local market that brings four fundamental risks.

01 · Credit dumping and “scorched earth”

Picture a local entrepreneur. He borrows at high interest rates, pays rent and fights for every customer. Then a branch of a global corporation opens next door.

The corporation has access to billions at home at minimal interest. It can afford to run deep in the red for two, three, five years in a row, simply undercutting prices.

Scenario Cheap money at home → years of dumping → local business goes bankrupt → the corporation becomes a monopoly → prices go up

This is exactly how Western chains scorched small retail in Eastern Europe in the 1990s.

02 · Money buys a one-way ticket

What happens when you buy coffee at a local café? The margin stays in the country. The owner goes and spends that money here: hires our contractors, buys milk from local farmers, pays for his children’s education. The money keeps working in the economy.

And when you take the same money to a global chain? The core margin (net profit and royalties) is converted into foreign currency and every quarter sent to headquarters accounts somewhere in Seattle or Paris. This is classic capital outflow.

One cup of coffee — two routes

Local café: your money → owner → contractors, farmer, school → back into the country’s economy.

Global chain: your money → profit and royalties → conversion into foreign currency → headquarters account abroad.

03 · Becoming a nation of cashiers

“But they create jobs!”

That is the most popular argument. They do. But which jobs? Strategy, marketing, product development (R&D) and top management with million-dollar bonuses stay in the brand’s home country for good. What we get are vacancies for cashiers, warehouse workers and couriers.

From the 1960s Mexico celebrated American maquiladora plants. In the end Mexicans spent decades assembling electronics from other people’s parts but never produced their own Apple or Samsung. You cannot become an inventor if you were hired to turn bolts according to someone else’s manual.

04 · Twisting the government’s arm

When a multinational becomes too big and employs thousands of people, it starts dictating terms to the state:

“Give us exclusive tax breaks, or we will close the plants and you will have a social explosion.”

Our mid-sized businesses never have that luxury or that influence, and end up on an uneven playing field.

Part 02

So what do we need? We need smart money

Don’t get me wrong: this is not a call to close the borders. Isolationism is a direct road to poverty. The country badly needs foreign capital. But it has to come in differently.

The real driver of growth is when foreign investors, funds or banks put money into our own, local companies.

What changes when capital enters a domestic company

  • The company stays ours
  • Profits are reinvested here
  • Taxes on high margins stay in our budget
  • Founders get not only money but world-class expertise, while keeping decision-making at home

After the war they did not let American corporations in. They took foreign loans and passed them to their own entrepreneurs — the chaebol (Samsung, Hyundai). These bought Western patents and hired the best engineers but remained 100% Korean companies. Today the whole world sends its money to Seoul.

When foreigners invested in their local startup Skype and later bought it for billions, the Estonian developers did not go off to sip cocktails on tropical islands. They became angel investors for their own compatriots. That money later grew Bolt, Wise and dozens of other global companies.

The flywheel effect Foreign capital into a local startup → successful exit → founders and developers become angels → new startups at home → a national business elite

Conclusion

Time to grow up

It is time to stop applauding other people’s signs.

The state’s job is not to roll out red carpets for multinational giants for the sake of PR photos. The real goal is to create such rock-solid rules of the game and such protection of private property that billions of dollars from investment funds want to flow into our factories, our technology companies and our brands.

Only then will the economy stop being a cheap service hub and start generating real wealth for its citizens.

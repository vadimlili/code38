Conspiracy theory vs reality

Why “expiring money” would be economic suicide

Taking apart the most popular fear about central bank digital currencies from the standpoint of basic economics — simply, with examples, without panic.

≈ 10 min read Analysis Macroeconomics Author: vadim.li · vadim.li@gmail.com

The text may be used only with the author’s permission. Inquiries: vadim.li@gmail.com

A CBDC (central bank digital currency) is an electronic equivalent of ordinary cash, held directly at the central bank without a commercial bank as an intermediary. The digital hryvnia, the digital euro, the digital yuan — these are all CBDCs.

A whole cloud of fears has grown around the topic. The most popular one goes like this:

“They will pay our wages in digital money with an ‘expiry date.’ Didn’t spend it by the end of the month — the money vanishes from your account. It’s a way to turn us into slaves of the state.”

Let’s examine this argument not from a moral standpoint but through cold economic logic — and see why such a system would be a catastrophe above all for the state itself.

Part 01

The three functions of money — and why none can be removed

Every economics textbook, from Adam Smith to today’s Nobel laureates, says the same: money works only when it performs three functions at once.

# Function What it means
1Medium of exchangeIt is used to pay for goods and services
2Unit of accountIt measures what things cost
3Store of valueIt can be put aside for the future without losing value

An analogy

Think of money as a battery. You worked, “charged” it with your labor, and want to use it a year from now — for a car, your child’s education, an apartment.

Now imagine the battery discharges on its own if you don’t use it right away. What do you do? Right — you throw it away and find another one. That is exactly what happens to a currency that loses its third function.

In Weimar Germany (1921–1923) hyperinflation destroyed the mark’s function as a store of value. People were paid twice a day and rushed to spend it immediately — not because they wanted to, but because purchasing power fell within hours. The result: the economy collapsed and political chaos followed. The state that allowed it lost everything.

Part 02

Banks, loans and the death of investment

Capitalism rests on a simple cycle: some people save, others invest. You put money on deposit. The bank lends to an entrepreneur who wants to open a bakery. The bakery hires people, pays taxes, produces goods. This is the classic macroeconomic identity: Saving = Investment (S = I).

What happens if money “expires”

  • Nobody opens a deposit, because the money has to be spent by the end of the month
  • Banks have no resources for long-term lending
  • An entrepreneur cannot get a loan to open a bakery, a factory or an IT startup
  • No new jobs are created — the economy turns into living from paycheck to paycheck

Take construction, one of the main engines of any country’s economy. Building a single apartment block takes steel, cement, electricity, machinery, architects, builders, furniture and plumbing manufacturers. All of them are paid from money the buyer borrowed on a mortgage or saved for years.

No savings → no mortgages → no buyers → construction dies → steel, building materials, furniture → an army of unemployed → social instability

Part 03

The “29th of the month” effect — how forced spending causes inflation

In theory, “expiring money” is supposed to stimulate consumption. In practice it creates an artificial spike in demand.

Imagine

Everyone’s money expires on the 30th of every month. What happens on the 29th? Everyone rushes to the shops at once. Shelves empty instantly. Sellers, seeing the frenzy, raise prices — first twofold, then fivefold.

This is a classic inflationary shock: there is plenty of money in the economy, but no more goods.

What’s more, people will start buying things they don’t need just to “save” the nominal value of their funds. This is an irrational allocation of resources: the economy produces what nobody needs because the system forces people into pointless purchases. The result is not economic growth but economic distortion.

Part 04

Why it doesn’t pay for the state: the argument from cold pragmatism

A common objection: “The state doesn’t need free consumers — it needs obedient slaves.” Let’s test this claim not on moral grounds but on grounds of self-interest.

A slave is the least efficient “employee” in history

A slave works exactly enough to avoid punishment. He will not design a new processor, develop efficient energy or write complex code. Innovation needs free, motivated people who have something to lose and something to strive for.

The Soviet Union — history’s largest experiment in building an economy without market incentives — lost the technology race to market economies. Not because its people were less intelligent, but because the system gave them no incentive to create something better.

The math of power doesn’t add up

To control a population with nothing to lose, the state needs a huge apparatus of repression: guards, censors, security forces. All of that costs money. But there is no money, because the economy has shrunk, businesses have closed and the tax base has vanished.

The state spends more on control than it gets from a ruined economy. The system destroys itself.

A person with a mortgage is the ideal citizen

A person with a 20-year mortgage, a good job and vacation plans is the most stable citizen. He has something to lose, so he is not inclined to radical action. A person whose money “expires” and who owns nothing is an unstable element. Every state instinctively fears that situation.

Geopolitical suicide

If country A introduces “slave money” and country B keeps a market economy, in 10 years country B will have modern technology, a strong army and influence, while country A will have a degrading population and outdated infrastructure. A state’s competitiveness depends directly on the economic freedom of its citizens.

Part 05

Currency competition — the final argument

Even if one state dared to try such an experiment, people always have alternatives:

  • Foreign cash
  • Gold and precious metals
  • Real estate
  • Cryptocurrencies
  • As a last resort, barter (cigarettes or ammunition have served as currency in times of crisis before)

People adapt instantly, settling in any assets that don’t lose value. A state that introduces “expiring money” will not gain control — it will lose control of financial flows altogether, driving the economy into the shadows.

Reality

What CBDCs actually are

Central banks are developing CBDCs for specific practical purposes:

  • Faster payments — transactions without delays or intermediaries
  • Lower costs — producing and handling cash is expensive
  • Fighting the shadow economy — transparent transactions make money laundering harder
  • Financial inclusion — in developing countries millions of people have no bank account

Real CBDC projects — the digital yuan (China), the digital euro (EU), the e-Naira (Nigeria) — nowhere include an “expiry” mechanism. On the contrary, central banks’ official documents explicitly commit to preserving all functions of money, including the store of value.

Bottom line

Theory vs reality

What the theory promises What actually happens
The state gains control over spending ✕ The state loses its tax base
Consumption rises ✕ An inflationary shock follows
Citizens become obedient ✕ Citizens move into the shadow economy
The economy speeds up ✕ Investment and jobs disappear

Without capital accumulation there is no investment. Without investment there is no growth. Without growth there is no state. This is not theory — it is math.

A CBDC is not a ration coupon from the era of rationing. It is digital money whose reliability the state has a vital interest in — just as with ordinary cash. Otherwise people will simply stop using it.

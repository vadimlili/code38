A country of sellers without a till: why Ukraine has been cut off for 15 years from PayPal and Stripe

Ukrainian makers list more on Etsy than Spain or Italy, yet they can’t get paid through PayPal. Moldova, with 2.4 million people, can. I look at who closed the doors, and how — from 2011 to today.

A craftswoman in Lviv sews dolls that people buy in California and Bavaria. Her Etsy shop has thousands of reviews. But when a buyer writes “can I pay with PayPal?”, she answers “no” — and loses the order. Her colleague in Chișinău answers “yes”. The difference between them isn’t talent, quality or the size of their country. The difference is fifteen years of decisions — some made in Kyiv, some in San Jose, and some in Moscow.

Part 01

The paradox: a market, but no till

The argument that “Ukraine is a small, uninteresting market” doesn’t survive contact with the platforms’ own numbers.

What’s more: in autumn 2022, already during the full-scale war, Ukraine had the world’s highest average sales per shop among countries with at least 10,000 shops [1]. Ukrainians know how to sell to the world. The problem is something else — how to get paid for it.

Part 02

The lost decade: 2011–2019

In the 2010s, online payments grew at a furious pace worldwide. By the end of that period PayPal operated in 190 of 193 countries, and residents of 116 of them could receive money for goods and services sold — including Malawi, where GDP per capita was $253 [15]. Ukraine didn’t make that list. And here the responsibility lies largely with the Ukrainian regulator.

Letters instead of dialoguefrom 2011

From 2011, the NBU’s leadership sent PayPal letters demanding that it go through the payment-system registration procedures and submit a document package described by the central bank — this became known from their 2015 correspondence [15]. PayPal replied that its dealings with Ukrainians matched international practice: in effect, the company simply accepted cards issued by Ukrainian banks, like any foreign website such as Amazon [16].

Your own account abroad — a violationuntil 2019

Until 2019, a Ukrainian could not legally keep money in their own account abroad without an individual NBU licence — liability for this had existed since 1993 [17]. For PayPal this meant: even if the company enabled receiving payments, the currency couldn’t be withdrawn to a Ukrainian card without an NBU licence [18]. As a result, around 20,000 Ukrainians opened PayPal business accounts through banks in neighbouring countries — and most of that money never reached Ukraine [19].

Half a step2016

On 10 February 2016, NBU Resolution No. 65 allowed individuals to receive e-money from non-residents — provided it was withdrawn to an account at a Ukrainian bank [20]. At the same time, the regulator insisted that only a bank could issue e-money [21]. In November 2016 the NBU announced that PayPal would not come to Ukraine within the next 12 months. According to a participant in the talks, the company’s representatives explained that they had limited human and time resources and their own two-year market-entry plan — “there are countries we can enter, and ones we can’t” [22].

The window opens2019

On 7 February 2019, the new currency law came into force: individual licences for transfers to one’s own account abroad were abolished, and individuals gained the right to transfer up to €50,000 a year [23]. Eight years after the NBU’s first letters, the main legal barrier was finally removed.

“An Iron Curtain we installed for ourselves”

That is how Ilia Kenigshtein, founder of the Creative States coworking chain and initiator of the movement to bring PayPal to Ukraine, described the situation. In his words, PayPal’s arrival was blocked by the National Bank’s restrictions and by oligarchs [24]. This is an activist’s assessment, not an established fact — but it shows how entrepreneurs themselves saw the situation.

Part 03

The 2019–2022 window: the rules changed, the companies didn’t come

After 2019, the state did what entrepreneurs had demanded for years. In 2020 the Ministry of Digital Transformation began talks with PayPal and Stripe [25], and Mykhailo Fedorov publicly promised that “Stripe will definitely be in Ukraine” [26]. In June 2021 the Rada adopted a payment services law based on the European PSD2 rules [27], which took effect on 1 August 2022 [28].

But then something emerged that changes the whole picture: according to the NBU, neither Stripe nor PayPal ever applied to the regulator for a licence or any other permit [29]. After two years of talks, the Ministry of Digital Transformation even asked Stripe for a roadmap with dates — and was waiting for an answer [30]. In other words, once Ukraine removed its own barriers, it turned out that the Ukrainian market had never become a priority for the companies.

The companies themselves partly explained this back in 2016: they have limited resources, and there are countries they “can enter, and ones they can’t” [22]. Ukraine, with its history of currency controls, the 2014–2015 crises and — since 2014 — occupied territories, ended up on the second list.

Part 04

The factor official statements stay silent on: the geography of sanctions

There is a reason that both our government and the companies rarely mention, but which directly affects the decisions of American fintechs. Part of Ukraine’s territory is under US sanctions because Russia occupied it.

As early as January 2015, PayPal stopped operating in Crimea to comply with Western sanctions [31]. Stripe explicitly lists Crimea among the regions it may not serve because of OFAC sanctions — alongside Cuba, Iran and North Korea [32]. After 2022 the list grew. The payments company Checkout.com explains to its clients that processing payments from Ukraine is normally allowed as long as they aren’t linked to Crimea, Donetsk, Luhansk, Kherson or Zaporizhzhia — and that this requires geolocation, postcode checks and manual review of Ukrainian payments [33].

What this means for business

For an American company, every client from Ukraine means an extra check: are they from an occupied territory? A mistake risks fines from the US regulator. Lithuania and Moldova don’t have this problem. So when a company compares “40 million Ukrainians” with “2.4 million Moldovans”, it counts not only the number of customers but also the cost and risk of checking each one. This is my conclusion from the facts above, not the companies’ official position — but that is exactly why it matters.

Part 05

Why the Baltics and Moldova can, and we can’t

The Baltics: EU membership

Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania have Stripe and full PayPal not because their market is more profitable. They are part of the EU single market, where a financial licence obtained in one country is valid in all the others. Stripe works in most EU countries [5] — a company doesn’t need to enter each one separately.

Moldova: a state that cleared the road

Moldova got PayPal at the same time as Belarus, Montenegro, Nigeria and other markets [34] and became the first CIS country where users could receive transfers via PayPal. Money withdrawn to Moldovan accounts was simply visible to, and taxed by, the tax service [35]. And on 29 April 2025, Moldova’s government approved rules that make it easier for businesses to use Stripe, PayPal and Revolut to receive payments from abroad and that relax the rules for repatriating currency up to 100,000 lei [36]. In other words, the state there didn’t wait for the companies to come — it removed its own barriers from their path.

Ukraine: one approach for everyone — and equally hard for everyone

The Ukrainian regulator holds a consistent position: the rules are the same for everyone, and any company providing financial services to Ukrainian residents must be authorised under Ukrainian law [37]. That is exactly where Revolut stopped, having served Ukrainians under a Lithuanian licence [38]. The position is legally impeccable — but as a result, for a global company Ukraine is a separate market with a separate procedure, wartime currency controls and sanctioned regions. Together, these outweigh 40 million potential customers.

Part 06

The war closed what had managed to open

The full-scale invasion of 2022 both opened and closed doors. On the one hand, at the Ukrainian government’s request PayPal for the first time allowed Ukrainians to receive transfers — before that they could only send money [39]. Etsy, which since 2021 had not let Ukrainians open new shops [40], launched Etsy Payments for them in October 2023 [41].

On the other hand, there are wartime currency controls which, according to KSE economists, restrict capital movement and profit repatriation and deter foreign companies [6]. Wise gradually wound down its services, explaining that the war makes it hard to guarantee a safe service [6]. Revolut gathered around 40,000 Ukrainian clients in its first week and 260,000 on the waitlist — and left because it had no Ukrainian licence [6].

A separate layer is the interests of the local market. monobank co-founder Oleh Horokhovskyi called Revolut’s exit “a banal violation of Ukrainian laws” [42], though he stressed he would be glad to see Revolut in Ukraine if it respected the law [43]. According to DOU, large banks tend to see foreign players as competitors, smaller ones as partners [6].

Part 07

Who is responsible — honestly

The regulator of the 2010s. For eight years — from the first letters in 2011 to the 2019 currency law — Ukraine lived under rules that made keeping your own money in an account abroad without a licence a violation. Precisely when global online payments were growing fastest.

The companies themselves. Once Ukraine removed the barriers, neither PayPal nor Stripe ever applied for a licence [29]. For them Ukraine is not a priority, and that is a business decision, not a conspiracy.

The government of the 2020s. Promises that “Stripe will definitely come” without a public roadmap and without a report on what exactly the companies object to. Meanwhile Moldova was adopting specific rules for Stripe and PayPal [36].

Russia. The occupation of Crimea since 2014 and of new territories since 2022 made Ukraine a country with sanctioned regions — and therefore more expensive and riskier for any American fintech [33].

Part 08

What could actually change the situation

1. EU membership. The payment services law is already built on PSD2. Once a European licence is valid in Ukraine too, entering our market will be a technical option for Stripe or Revolut rather than a separate project — as in the Baltics.

2. Do what Moldova did. Don’t wait for the companies — remove barriers from their path: simplified rules for receiving payments from abroad and repatriating currency, clear taxation.

3. Continue currency liberalisation. Since 11 August 2026, the limit on paying for goods and services abroad from FX accounts is 200,000 hryvnias a month [14]. The more predictable the rules for moving money out, the lower the risk for companies.

4. A public roadmap. An open list of each company’s requirements and of which ones the state has already met — instead of promises that it “will definitely come”.

What works right now

Etsy — via Etsy Payments and Payoneer [11]. Payoneer — by experts’ estimates, around 30% of Ukrainian freelancers were already using it in 2021 [24]. Interactive Brokers — accounts for Ukrainian residents [13]. PayPal — for personal transfers [7]. For digital goods, Ukrainian Gumroad alternatives with payment in hryvnias are appearing [44].
